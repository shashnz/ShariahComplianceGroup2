Shariah Compliance in Islamic Banking

Explore how Islamic financial institutions ensure Shariah compliance in their products, governance and daily operations while examining the principles and sources of Islamic law and real cases in Malaysia.

1Introduction

Understanding Shariah-Compliant Banking

Islamic banks are Shariah-compliant financial institutions that strictly follow Islamic law across their processes and governance. Unlike conventional systems that view money as a commodity to earn profit through interest, Islamic banking treats money purely as a medium of exchange. To preserve religious and business credibility, modern Islamic banks rely on a two-layer governance framework that combines an internal Shariah Supervisory Board with a central regulatory authority. This is to ensure operations drive genuine entrepreneurship and sustainable economic value (Ahmed, 2014).

Halal – Haram Foundation

Islamic finance's core philosophy is to avoid Haram activities. This requires the absolute removal of three fundamental prohibitions: Riba (predetermined returns on loans without risk), Gharar (deception, asymmetric information, or hidden contract terms), and Maysir (excessive speculation in which wealth is created by chance rather than effort). By removing these elements, the market is redefined based on mutual consent and shared risk (Dusuki & Abdullah, 2007).

The Barakah Concept

The Philosophy of Barakah is the spiritual and ethical foundation of Islamic finance, encompassing Az-Ziadah (moral growth and continuous wealth) and As-Sa'adah (true peace and societal welfare). Under this concept, financial institutions must look beyond short-term profit maximisation to prevent exploitation, monopoly, and wealth concentration. Islamic banks support public welfare through integrating social safety nets such as Zakat, Waqf, and risk-sharing investments, thereby combining spiritual ethics with real-world economic responsibility (AIMS Education, 2026).

2Main Discussion

Sources, framework, and features of Shariah compliance

Compliance with Shariah law is based on an elaborate system of sources of Islamic law backed by a broad governance structure used in Islamic financial institutions. This ensures that all banking transactions, products and services are undertaken according to Islamic principles while remaining differently characterized from conventional systems of banking (Dusuki & Abdullah, 2007).

2.1 Sources of Shariah

Primary Sources

The primary sources for Shariah are the foundation precepts of Islamic law, serving as the main references for Islamic finance principles. Any idea, practice, or act in Islamic finance must be in agreement with primary sources of Islamic law (Kamali, 2008).

Al-Qur'an

Al-Quran is the highest and most authoritative source of Shariah, providing important guidance in economic affairs. It dictates primary rules for commercial transactions, including the prohibition of riba, and stresses the importance of lawful transactions and justice in commerce (Iqbal & Mirakhor, 2011).

As-Sunnah

As-Sunnah pertains to the beliefs, traditions, and sayings of Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) that shed light on the rules mentioned in Al-Qur’an. It is the source of practical knowledge on how to conduct business and deal with people ethically, and how to carry out financial transactions in accordance with Shariah principles. (Kamali,2008).

Secondary Sources

Secondary Shariah sources serve as supportive references created by Islamic scholars to interpret and apply Shariah principles in relation to contemporary issues, which includes modern finance transactions and banking activities. Such sources help scholars make correct decisions where the primary sources do not give specific instructions (BNM, 2019).

Ijma' (consensus)

Ijma’ is defined as a consensus achieved by qualified Shariah jurists on a legal issue at hand. It is a collective view of experts in a particular subject matter, who are called upon to assist when the matter cannot be resolved using the basic sources of Shariah (BNM, 2019).

Qiyas (analogy)

Qiyas is understood as the application of the Sharia ruling in an existing situation, which is considered similar due to the existence of an ‘illah factor. This instrument allows one to apply Islamic law to new situations that have emerged in the financial sector (BNM, 2019).

Istihsan (juristic preference)

Istihsan is seen as a situation where one chooses a better legal ruling as a result of too strict application of the existing legal rule leading to difficulties, hardship or injustices. The aim in using this method of ruling is to achieve justice and ensure that the objectives of Shariah are attained in the sphere of finance (BNM, 2019).

'Urf (custom)

‘Urf is defined as accepted traditions and customs in a society which can be taken into account while issuing Islamic rulings if they do not contradict any established rule of Shariah. This instrument allows flexibility in dealing with local customs and market practices that are not addressed by primary sources of Shariah.

Istishab (presumption of continuity)

The principle of Istishab states that one must hold an existing legal position or ruling unless there is clear evidence proving that it has changed. It creates certainty in cases where there is no occurrence of distinct evidence through which new ruling can be established. (Kamali, 2008)

Maslahah al-Mursalah (public interest)

Maslahah refers to the concept of welfare, as well as public interest and avoidance of damage in determining Islamic rulings. The concept of Maslahah is of immense importance where new issues arise that are not governed by the textual sources of Shariah (Kamali, 2008).

Sadd al-Zara'i (blocking the means)

Sadd al-Zara’i refers to the prohibition of an action that is otherwise permissible based on the underlying concern that it could potentially result in an unlawful act. The purpose of this principle is to safeguard individuals and humanity from any act that could potentially be contrary to the tenets of Shariah (Kamali, 2008).

Qaul as-Sahabi (companions' fatwa)

Qaul denotes the scholarly opinion offered by the competent Islamic scholar on any Shariah issue. Scholarly opinions help in finding solutions to issues that revolve around the interpretation of Shariah law and deal with nascent and complex issues pertaining to Islamic law and finance (Kamali, 2008).

2.2 The Shariah Governance Framework

Shariah Advisory Council (SAC)

The Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) is the highest Shariah authority in Malaysia’s Islamic financial system. Established under Bank Negara Malaysia, it provides authoritative rulings and guidance on Shariah matters to ensure the consistent application of Islamic principles across the industry. Its decisions serve as an important reference for Islamic financial institutions and help strengthen public confidence in the Islamic financial system (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2019).

In-House Shariah Committee

The In-House Shariah Committee is an independent body within an Islamic financial institution that oversees Shariah compliance in its operations. It advises the board and management on Shariah matters, reviews business activities, and monitors compliance with Shariah requirements. Through continuous supervision, the committee helps ensure that the institution operates ethically, transparently, and in accordance with Islamic principles (BNM, 2019; Islamic Financial Services Board, 2020).

2.4 Key Elements That Keep a Bank Shariah Compliant

Shariah
values

Real Economic Activities

Every transaction must be linked to a real underlying asset and not just generate profit from money. For example, Maybank Islamic Commodity Murabahah Home Financing-i uses the Murabahah concept, where all home financing is structured through the trading of Shariah-compliant commodities such as crude palm oil and RBD Palm Olein. (Maybank Islamic, 2026a).

Ethics

The operations of Islamic banks must not involve prohibited elements such as riba (usury), gharar (uncertainty), or maysir (gambling); instead, they emphasise justice, information disclosure, and social safety nets such as zakat and waqf. This ensures that all financial activities remain grounded in Shariah principles. For example, regarding the Maybank Islamic Commodity Murabahah Home Financing-I product, Maybank states that the offering utilises the Murabahah principle specifically by not imposing compounded profit or other charges to maintain transparency in Islamic financial transactions (Maybank Islamic, 2026a).

Governance

Islamic banking is characterised by a high level of transparency, the implementation of Shariah audits, and fiduciary accountability at every level, all of which ensure that banking operations remain compliant with Shariah principles. In Malaysia, for instance, the Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) of Bank Negara Malaysia serves as the highest Shariah authority in Islamic finance. It is responsible for determining Shariah rulings regarding Islamic business activities and validating products to ensure their alignment with Shariah principles (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2026b).

Partnership

Risks and rewards are shared between the bank and the customer, rather than being transferred to a single party. This principle demonstrates that both parties share returns and risks based on the Shariah contract employed. For instance, Maybank Islamic offers the Shophouse Equity-i product, which utilizes the Musharakah Mutanaqisah concept. Under this arrangement, the bank and the customer share ownership of the asset, while the customer gradually purchases the bank's share of ownership until they fully own the asset (Maybank Islamic, 2026b).

•Regulatory Timeline

Evolution of Shariah governance in Malaysia

Malaysia's Shariah compliance framework was developed gradually over four decades with each regulation introduced to address gaps in the earlier ones.

1983

Islamic Banking Act (IBA)

The development of the Shariah governance framework in Malaysia started with the Islamic Banking Act (IBA) 1983, which gave the statutory framework for Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) to license and supervise the first dedicated Islamic banking institution in Malaysia, Bank Islam Malaysia Berhad (BIMB). This was the first law to regulate Islamic banking operations alongside conventional banking. It gave BNM the authority to oversee BIMB and also required Islamic financial institutions to set up internal Shariah advisory bodies to make sure their daily operation followed Islamic law (Muhammad, 2011).

1997

Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) Established

In 1997, Bank Negara Malaysia set up the Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) as the highest national authority on Islamic banking and takaful. It was formed to address conflicts and inconsistencies in how Shariah was being interpreted across financial institutions (Bank Negara Malaysia, n.d.). The SAC’s rulings later gained explicit statutory backing under the Central Bank of Malaysia Act 2009, making them legally binding in both civil courts and arbitral tribunals (Bank Negara Malaysia, n.d.; Calder, 2020).

2010

Shariah Governance Framework (SGF)

The Islamic finance industry grew more complex, so BNM launched the Shariah Governance Framework (SGF) in 2010 to standardise internal controls for Islamic Financial Institutions (IFIs). It introduced four pillars: an independent Shariah Committee, internal Shariah review, periodic Shariah audit, and Shariah risk management and made the Board of Directors and senior management directly accountable. Shariah compliance was no longer just about approving products; it became part of the bank’s overall governance (Muhammad, 2011; Omar & Hassan, 2019).

2013

Islamic Financial Services Act (IFSA)

The Islamic Financial Services Act (IFSA) 2013 consolidated and replaced the IBA 1983 and the Takaful Act 1984 into a single law covering banking, takaful, and money market regulations. It made the Shariah Advisory Council’s rulings legally binding in courts and arbitration. IFSA also introduced strict penalties including fines and imprisonment, for non-compliance with Shariah, and set clear rules on classifying products by contract type to maintain Shariah integrity throughout the process (Mohamed & Ibrahim, 2023; Omar & Hassan, 2019).

2019

Shariah Governance Policy Document (SGPD)

In response to international corporate governance standards, BNM issued the Shariah Governance Policy Document (SGPD) 2019, effective 1 April 2020 to replace the 2010 SGF and strengthen institutional accountability (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2019). The SGPD gave the Board of Directors primary responsibility for Shariah governance. It also introduced strict independence, objectivity, and tenure limits for Shariah Committee members. Beyond that, financial institutions were required to build an enterprise-wide “Shariah compliance culture” that runs through all business strategies and daily operations (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2019; Mohamed & Ibrahim, 2023).

3 • Critical Analysis

Critical Challenges and Comtempory Issues Shaping Modern Islamic Finance

A closer look at how digital innovation, ethical dilemmas and market benchmarks affect Islamic banking today.

Issue 01 ⚡️

Digital & Fintech Pressure

FinTech innovations like digital wallets, digital onboarding processes, Robo Advisory and crowdfunding have transformed Islamic banking. Despite making the work easier, these advancements complicate Shariah compliance. All Islamic banks need to ensure that all their technological products and transactions pass through the Shariah approval process before use. Due to the fast-paced changes in the technological world, there is a need for strong partnership among regulators, Shariah experts, and technologists. (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2019).

Issue 02 ⚖️

Shariah-Compliant vs Based

A key challenge in Islamic finance is differentiating between Shariah-compliant and Shariah-based instruments. The Shariah-compliant instruments meet the legal requirements of Islamic law, while the Shariah-based instruments align with the underlying objectives of the Shariah such as justice, equity, transparency and risk sharing. The extensive use of Tawarruq has alarmed some scholars as it resembles debt financing. Hence, Islamic financial institutions must develop more partnership-oriented products. (International Shariah Research Academy for Islamic Finance, 2020).

Issue 03 📈

Benchmark & Pricing Dependency

Despite being non-interest-bearing, Islamic banks still adopt conventional benchmarks like BFR and KLIBOR to measure their financing products. While this maintains market competitiveness and pricing consistency, it causes public confusion regarding the distinctiveness of Islamic financial institutions. Therefore, this is why most researchers strongly advocate for Islamic-based pricing benchmarks. (Accounting and Auditing Organisation for Islamic Financial Institutions, 2023).

4 • Real-Life Application

Islamic Financial Contracts in Practice

How core Shariah principles are structured into everyday modern banking products.

01. Profit-Sharing Partnership

Mudharabah Investment

A profit-sharing contract where the customer provides capital and the bank manages the investment. Profits are shared based on an agreed ratio, but losses are borne by the capital provider unless caused by the bank’s negligence. Commonly used in Islamic investment accounts as it reflects the risk-sharing principle central to Islamic finance (BNM, 2013).

📌 Applied in: Investment accounts, term deposits, and wealth management.

02. Cost-Plus Sale

Murabahah Financing

A sale-and-purchase contract where the bank buys an asset first, then resells it to the customer at cost plus an agreed profit margin, avoiding gharar since the price is known upfront. Widely used for vehicle, equipment, and business financing via instalments, ensuring the transaction is asset-based rather than an interest-bearing loan (BNM, 2013; AAOIFI, 2015).

📌 Applied in: Home financing, auto financing, and asset acquisitions.

03. Commodity Murabahah

Tawarruq Monetization

A contract involving buying a commodity on deferred payment, then reselling it to a third party for cash. Widely used in personal financing and Islamic credit cards in Malaysia, but closely monitored by the Shariah Committee to ensure it isn’t just a workaround for interest-based loans (BNM, 2013; IFSB, 2020).

📌 Applied in: Personal financing, Islamic credit cards, and liquidity management.

5 REAL CASE / CURRENT ISSUE

Disclosure of Shariah Non-Compliance (SNC) Income by Malaysian Islamic Banks

Even well-managed Islamic banks may occasionally record Shariah non-compliant (SNC) income due to operational errors or unforeseen circumstances. From a Shariah governance perspective, what matters most is how the income is identified, disclosed transparently, and purified according to Bank Negara Malaysia's requirements (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2019).

RM19.2m

Total Shariah non-compliant income reported by Malaysian Islamic banks between 2015 and 2020

2016

The year with the highest reported Shariah non-compliance income during the study period

What this shows

Shariah non-compliance does not necessarily indicate weak governance. Instead, transparent identification, disclosure, and purification of SNC income reflect an effective Shariah governance framework and strong internal controls. This practice enhances accountability, protects stakeholders' interests and preserves public trust in Malaysia's Islamic banking industry (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2019; Mohamed & Ibrahim, 2023).

Beyond Compliance: Core Ethical Insights & Professional Value

Through the production of this e-Portfolio for the Islamic Banking and Financial System Subject, we have gained very deep knowledge of finance such as knowing what the basic principles are, the governance framework, and the practical applications of Islamic muamalat. This assignment reveals how the Islamic financial system is not only an alternative to conventional banking, but rather it is a separate system based on socio-economic justice, transparency and real economic activity.

Among the main lessons learned is the importance of avoiding prohibited elements such as Riba (interest), Gharar (uncertainty) and Maysir (gambling) in addition to appreciating the concepts of Barakah and Maqasid Shariah. Through observations of financial contracts such as Mudarabah, Murabahah and Tawarruq, we can understand that every transaction must be backed by physical assets or real trade. In addition, analysis of the regulatory evolution from the Islamic Banking Act 1983, IFSA 2013, to the Shariah Governance Policy Document (SGPD 2019) by Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) has proven that Shariah integrity can be upheld through strict internal controls and the important role of the Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) in the Internal Shariah Committee.

Furthermore, from an ethical and professional perspective, this assignment has opened our eyes to the fact that syariah compliance is not just about fulfilling legal requirements, but is also a moral and fiduciary responsibility. For example, the 19.2 million syariah illicit earnings (SNC) laundering case by Islamic banks has shown how important the principles of transparency and accountability are to maintaining public trust.

Finally, in the future, the rapid development of financial technology (Fintech) and the challenges of reliance on conventional benchmark rates (such as KLIBOR) require Islamic finance practitioners to continue to innovate without compromising ethical values. This knowledge gained provides a solid foundation for us to apply the principles of integrity, ethical decision-making and the values ​​of professionalism in our future financial careers.
